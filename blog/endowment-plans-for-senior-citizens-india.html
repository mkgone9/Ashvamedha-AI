Endowment Plans for Senior Citizens: Worth It After 50?

✍️ Manoj Kumar📅 November 2026⏱️ 8 min read📍 Ashvamedha Finance, Hyderabad
📚 Part of: Retirement & Parents guide →

Every month, someone asks me: "My father is 58. Should we take an endowment policy for him?" The honest answer is: it depends on what the money is for. For some seniors an endowment or guaranteed plan is a smart choice; for many others, there are simpler, better options. Here is how to decide.

The short answer

  • Most endowment / guaranteed savings plans accept entry up to around 55–60 years, and the life cover is costlier at that age.
  • If a senior needs income now, SCSS (8.2%), POMIS (7.4%) or an immediate annuity usually beat an endowment plan.
  • An endowment or guaranteed plan makes sense at 50–60 for tax-free income later, a legacy for children, or locking today's returns for decades.

What an endowment plan is

An endowment plan is a life insurance policy that pays a lump sum on maturity (or to the nominee if the person dies earlier). A guaranteed income plan is a close cousin: instead of one lump sum, it pays a fixed amount every year for a long period, often with the premiums returned at the end.

Both are "non-linked" (no market risk) and the benefits are written into the policy on day one.

Why endowment is tricky after 55

Entry age limitsMany plans allow entry only up to 50–60 years; terms shrink as age rises.
Costlier life coverA part of every premium pays for life cover. At 58 it costs much more than at 35, which lowers the return.
Medical underwritingDiabetes, BP or heart history can mean tests, a higher premium or a decline.
Long lock-inMoney is tied up for 10–20 years. Surrendering early returns less than you paid.

So a traditional endowment plan bought at 60 for "returns" usually works out poorer than simpler options. That doesn't make every such plan a bad idea; it means the purpose decides.

When it does make sense

  • You are 45–58, still earning, and want a tax-free income from your mid-60s that doesn't depend on future interest rates.
  • Your SCSS limit is full (₹30 Lakh per person) and you want another safe, locked income source.
  • You are in a high tax slab and taxable FD interest is eating your return.
  • Legacy: you want a defined sum to pass to children, with a nominee and quick claim settlement.
  • A child is paying the premium for a parent in their 50s, to build that parent's later-life income.
🧠 Remember it: "Now-money in SCSS, later-money in a locked plan." Use government schemes for today's expenses, a guaranteed plan for the income you'll need in 12–15 years.

How to choose one for a parent

  1. Check entry age and premium-paying term. A 10-year pay plan at 55 means paying until 65. Is that comfortable?
  2. Look at income, not just "sum assured". Ask: how much will I get each year, from when, for how long?
  3. Disclose all health conditions. Non-disclosure is the most common reason claims are disputed.
  4. Keep total yearly premium ≤ ₹5 Lakh across such policies to keep payouts tax-free.
  5. Compare with buying in your own name. If you (the child) are much younger, a plan in your name often gives more income and higher cover, and you pass the income to your parent.

Frequently asked questions

Can a 60-year-old buy an endowment plan?

Some plans accept entry up to about 60 (a few go higher), but the choice is limited, medical tests are more likely and the return is lower because life cover costs more at that age.

What is the best investment for senior citizens for regular income?

For income starting now: the Senior Citizens Savings Scheme (8.2%), Post Office MIS (7.4%), senior citizen FDs and immediate annuities. A guaranteed income plan suits income needed 12+ years later.

Is endowment plan maturity taxable for senior citizens?

For policies issued after 1 April 2023, maturity is tax-free only if the total yearly premium across such policies is ₹5 Lakh or less (and the premium is within 10% of the sum assured). Otherwise, the gain is taxable.

Should I buy the plan in my parent's name or mine?

If you are much younger, buying in your own name usually gives more income per rupee and higher life cover; you can pass the income to your parent. A plan in the parent's name gives them direct ownership.

Is LIC or a private insurer better for seniors?

Look at the guaranteed income, claim settlement record, service and flexibility rather than the brand alone. Get the official benefit illustration for the exact age before deciding.
