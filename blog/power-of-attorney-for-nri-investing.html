Power of Attorney for NRI Investing — Do You Need One?

✍️ Manoj Kumar📅 September 2026⏱️ 7 min read📍 Ashvamedha Finance, Hyderabad

Not every NRI needs a POA, but for specific, recurring situations, it genuinely helps. Here's when it matters and how to set one up without unnecessary risk.

Why This Comes Up So Often for NRIs Specifically

Many routine investment and banking tasks in India still benefit from, or sometimes require, in-person presence or physical signatures — updating KYC details, handling certain property transactions, or managing time-sensitive paperwork. For an NRI who can't always be physically present in India when these needs arise, a Power of Attorney (POA) lets a trusted person act on their behalf for defined purposes.

What a POA Can Actually Authorize

This depends entirely on how the document is drafted — a POA can be scoped narrowly (e.g., "sign KYC update forms for my mutual fund folios") or broadly (e.g., general authority over most financial matters). The broader the scope, the more the holder can do — and the more risk exists if that trust is ever misplaced. Common NRI use cases include managing property-related paperwork, completing bank/demat KYC updates, and executing specific transactions the NRI has already decided on but can't be physically present to sign.

Specific vs General POA — The Real Trade-Off

TypeScopeRisk level
Specific/Limited POANamed, defined tasks only (e.g., "sell this specific property," "manage this specific demat account")Lower — holder can't act outside the defined scope
General POABroad authority across most financial/legal mattersHigher — meaningful potential for misuse if trust is misplaced

For most NRI investment purposes, a specific, narrowly scoped POA covering exactly the recurring tasks you need handled is the more prudent choice over a broad general POA, even when granted to someone you trust completely — narrow scope simply limits downside risk without meaningfully limiting what you actually need done.

How to Set One Up Properly

  1. Draft it with a lawyer, specifying exactly what's authorized — vague language creates ambiguity that can cause problems later, including with banks or registrars who may refuse to act on unclear authorization
  2. Execute and notarize it correctly — a POA executed abroad for use in India typically needs to be notarized and may require attestation by the Indian embassy/consulate in your country of residence, then adjudicated (stamped) in India within a specified time period
  3. Register it if required — POAs involving property transactions in India often need registration; check current requirements for your specific use case
  4. Share the specific document with relevant institutions — banks, AMCs, or registrars will need to see and verify the POA before acting on the holder's instructions

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Granting overly broad authority "just in case" — scope it to what you actually need
  • Not setting an expiry or review date — a POA that remains active indefinitely without periodic review is easy to forget about, even when circumstances (including trust in the holder) may have changed
  • Skipping proper attestation/notarization — an improperly executed POA may simply be rejected by Indian institutions when it's actually needed, defeating the purpose
  • Not informing the concerned institutions in advance — a POA sitting in a drawer, unknown to the relevant bank or registrar, doesn't help when a time-sensitive need arises

Do You Actually Need One?

If your investments are simple, don't involve property, and you're able to complete KYC and transactions digitally when needed (increasingly common with video KYC), you may not need a POA at all. If you own property in India, have complex or time-sensitive paperwork needs, or simply can't reliably be present for periodic requirements, a specific, well-drafted POA genuinely reduces friction — worth setting up proactively rather than scrambling when a need arises urgently.
